# Know Your People: Stakeholder Mapping and Managing Up in a Small Nonprofit

> **In one paragraph:** Your influence as a solo PM rests on relationships, not authority. Begin by listing everyone who can affect your work or is affected by it, and include the people with little formal power, especially the people you serve. Sort them by power and interest, then check the result for legitimacy and urgency so beneficiaries are not quietly pushed to the bottom. Give each group a communication rhythm that fits its place on the map. Put most of your relationship time into your Executive Director: learn their goals, pressures and preferred way of taking in information, never let them be surprised, and go through them to reach the board. Review the map every few months, because people's positions change.

## Why this matters when you're the only PM

In a small nonprofit, nobody reports to you, but almost everything you ship depends on someone else. The program lead decides whether staff actually use the new intake form. The development director knows what a funder was promised. A board member may have strong opinions about the website. The ED can stop any of it in one conversation.

The evidence that this matters is fairly strong. Bryson's widely cited review for public and nonprofit managers points to Paul Nutt's study of 400 strategic decisions. About half of those decisions failed, in large part because decision makers ignored the interests and information held by key stakeholders [1]. Stakeholder thinking started in business strategy (Freeman defined a stakeholder as anyone who can affect or is affected by an organization's objectives, as quoted in [1]). Bryson argues that public and nonprofit organizations should use a broader definition that includes the "nominally powerless" [1]. For a mission-driven organization, those are often the people you exist to serve.

When you're the only PM, nobody else is tracking these relationships for you. A light, regular routine is enough. You don't need a big analysis.

## Practical advice

1. **Start with a broad list, then narrow it.** Write down everyone who can affect your product or is affected by it: ED, board chair and any board members with a stake in it, program staff, development/fundraising, finance or operations, volunteers, beneficiaries/clients, funders, and partner organizations. Bryson recommends starting public and nonprofit stakeholder work with a broad definition and narrowing it later, because who you include decides whose interests count [1]. Ackermann, Eden and McKiernan's study of senior executives found that effective managers paid attention to internal stakeholders as well as external ones [4]. For you, that means front-desk staff and volunteer coordinators count as stakeholders too, alongside funders. *Example:* building a volunteer scheduling tool, you would list the volunteer coordinator, the volunteers, the program sites that rely on those volunteers, and the funder whose grant report counts volunteer hours.

2. **Sort the list with a power/interest grid, but treat it as a rough sketch.** The grid, usually credited to Mendelow's 1981 work on stakeholders and environmental scanning [2] and developed by Eden and Ackermann [1], sorts people into four groups. *Players* have high interest and high power. *Subjects* have high interest and low power. *Context setters* have high power and low interest. The *crowd* has little of either [1]. Project-management teaching attaches a communication approach to each group: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor [12]. A typical small-nonprofit version might look like this: ED and program director are players. Frontline staff and clients are subjects. The board treasurer and the major funder are context setters. The wider donor list is the crowd. Fifteen minutes on a whiteboard is enough.

3. **Check the grid for legitimacy and urgency so beneficiaries aren't treated as an afterthought.** Mitchell, Agle and Wood argue that stakeholder salience depends on legitimacy and urgency as well as power. They describe "dependent" stakeholders, who have a legitimate and urgent claim but no power of their own [3]. That describes many clients well. Twersky, Buchanan and Threlfall point out that beneficiaries usually didn't choose the service and don't pay for it. They often have few alternatives and may thank you even when it isn't working, so the organization gets little warning when it is failing them. Meanwhile, funders' priorities tend to win out [9]. In practice, mark beneficiaries as a group whose input you will actively go and get (short interviews, feedback forms, sitting in on a program session). Don't treat them as "low power, monitor." Bryson also notes that the same grid can be used to help the relatively powerless get heard [1].

4. **Give each quadrant a communication cadence, and write it down.** Agree on the rhythm before anyone needs it. A sustainable version for a solo PM looks like this. *ED:* a weekly 30-minute 1:1. *Other players* (program and development leads): every two weeks, or a standing slot in an existing meeting. *Context setters* (board, key funder contact): a short written update that goes through the ED, monthly or before each board meeting. *Subjects* (frontline staff, volunteers, clients): a monthly "what's changing" note and regular feedback sessions. *Crowd:* updates only when something affects them. The Open University's stakeholder engagement example sets update frequency by quadrant and expects the plan to change as the project goes on [12]. Mind the Product's practitioner advice says the same: communicate regularly and transparently [10].

5. **Make the ED relationship your top priority, and treat it as a two-way dependency.** Gabarro and Kotter's HBR classic describes the boss relationship as one of mutual dependence. Bosses rely on cooperation, reliability and honesty. You rely on them for connections, priorities and resources [5]. They recommend learning your boss's goals, pressures, strengths and work style, being honest with yourself about your own, and taking responsibility for clarifying expectations rather than assuming you know them [5]. Hiland's study of 18 board chair and ED pairs found trust was the most important factor. Strong pairs built it through ongoing dialogue and by learning each other's styles. Simply defining roles did not do it [8]. *In practice:* in your first 1:1 of the quarter, ask your ED what success looks like this quarter, what is keeping them up at night (often a grant renewal or a board concern), and how they want to hear about problems.

6. **Match how you share information to how your ED takes it in.** Gabarro and Kotter relay Drucker's distinction between "readers" and "listeners." If your boss is a reader, send the memo first and discuss it afterwards. If your boss is a listener, brief them in person and follow up in writing [5]. If your ED skims email but engages in conversation, a two-page product proposal will sit unread until you walk them through it.

7. **Practice "no surprises," especially with bad news.** Gabarro and Kotter warn that it is easy to shade the truth and play down issues, so today's concerns become tomorrow's surprise problems. They call dishonesty perhaps the most damaging trait a subordinate can have [5]. BoardSource lists a commitment to "no surprises," including candor about bad news, as a core practice of the board–staff partnership, together with regular check-ins between the executive and the chair [6]. Mind the Product makes the same point for PMs: rosy status reports destroy trust, and senior people help more when they know the real situation [10]. *Example:* if the donor-database migration will slip past the year-end appeal, tell your ED this week, along with options. Don't let them find out from the development director in December.

8. **Reach the board through the ED, not around them.** In BoardSource's model, the chief executive leads staff and supports the board. Board and staff share communication and trust but have separate lanes, summed up as "no micro-managing / no micro-governing" [7]. A solo PM who lobbies a board member directly about a product decision undermines the ED–board partnership that holds the organization together [6][7]. Hiland found some high-trust pairs where board chairs had frequent direct contact with staff, with the ED's support [8]. So the rule is to agree the channel with your ED. *In practice:* offer to draft the product section of the ED's board report, and ask whether it would help for you to present one item at a board meeting.

9. **Bring influential people in early, while change is still cheap.** SVPG advises showing stakeholders rapid prototypes early, so their feedback arrives while changes are still cheap [11]. Mind the Product recommends agreeing clear, prioritized goals at the start of any big effort [10]. Bryson describes "back room" preliminary stakeholder analysis by a small group before wider engagement [1]. *Example:* before the all-staff meeting where you present a new case-management workflow, walk the program director through a clickable mockup one-on-one so their concerns come up privately.

10. **Re-map each quarter, or whenever something big changes.** Mitchell, Agle and Wood describe power as variable, something that can be gained and lost [3]. Ackermann and colleagues found that how stakeholders respond to a proposed strategy changes the dynamics around it [4]. A new board chair, a lost grant or a new program director can move people across your grid overnight.

## Try this in the next two weeks

- [ ] List every stakeholder on one page (aim for 15–30 names or groups, including beneficiaries and volunteers).
- [ ] Place them on a power/interest grid, then circle anyone with a legitimate, urgent claim but little power.
- [ ] Write a one-line cadence next to each quadrant (who, how, how often).
- [ ] In your next ED 1:1, ask: "What does success look like this quarter? What worries you most? How do you want to hear bad news?"
- [ ] Ask your ED how they prefer to get information (reading or conversation) and adjust your next update to match.
- [ ] Agree with your ED how product topics reach the board.
- [ ] Find one piece of news your ED doesn't yet know and should, and tell them this week.
- [ ] Book 20-minute conversations with two program staff and two clients or volunteers.
- [ ] Put a recurring quarterly "re-map stakeholders" reminder in your calendar.

## Where this advice breaks down

- **The grid is a heuristic. It has not been tested as a method.** Bryson notes there are no firm rules, and little good empirical evidence, on how much participation is too much or too little [1]. Ackermann, Eden and McKiernan describe empirical stakeholder research with senior leaders as rare, and their own study covered only healthcare executives [4]. Use the grid to prompt thinking, not as proof.
- **Power-only maps can push out the people you serve.** Narrow definitions count only people who have power [1][3]. In a nonprofit that can quietly turn into "the funder decides," which is the drift Twersky and colleagues warn about [9].
- **Mapping can slide into manipulation.** Bryson acknowledges the grid can be used to decide who to "co-opt" [1]. Ethical influence means using the map to understand and include people. Write nothing on it you'd be embarrassed for the person to read.
- **Role rules aren't the whole story.** Hiland found that strict reliance on prescribed roles showed up only in low-trust relationships [8]. "Always go through the ED" is a sensible default, but it is something to agree with your ED, not a law.
- **Most sources describe bigger organizations.** Gabarro and Kotter wrote about corporate managers [5], and SVPG and Mind the Product write for tech product teams [10][11]. In a 12-person nonprofit, the ED may also be your only peer, your fundraiser, and the person who answers the phone. Weekly 1:1s may need to be 20 minutes. Fit the cadence to the capacity you actually have.
- **Citation note:** many secondary sources date Mendelow's grid to 1991. The verifiable original is his 1981 ICIS conference paper [2], and the grid as most people use it today owes much to Eden and Ackermann's later work [1].
