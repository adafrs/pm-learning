# Communicating for Influence: Clear Writing, Stories with Data, and Presenting to Leadership and the Board

> **In one paragraph:** As the only product person in a small nonprofit, most of your influence travels through what you write and say to your Executive Director (ED), your board, and your program colleagues. Lead with the answer, then the reasons (the Minto "pyramid"). Put important proposals in a short written memo rather than a slide deck. Pair a real, consented beneficiary story with an honest number, because statistics tend to shift beliefs and stories tend to shift intentions. Send short, regular updates so your work is visible before you need a decision. Talk to key people one-on-one before the big meeting, openly and without backroom deals. Be careful with the popular claim that "one story beats any statistic": recent replications suggest it is much weaker than nonprofit folklore assumes.

## Why this matters when you're the only PM

In a larger company, a product team has a director, a roadmap review process, and peers who carry its message into rooms it isn't in. You don't. Your ED is juggling funders, staff, and compliance. Your board meets a few times a year and reads your work in a packet alongside the audit and the gala plan. Program staff are stretched and may see "product" as an IT side project. Any of them can quietly stall a good idea simply because they didn't understand it, didn't see it coming, or didn't see why it mattered to the people you serve.

Clear communication doesn't manipulate anyone. It respects the scarce attention of people who volunteer their time (the board) or carry the whole organization (the ED). Getting it right is often the cheapest way to earn trust.

## Practical advice

**1. Put the answer first, then the reasons.** Barbara Minto's *Pyramid Principle* holds that a reader grasps your thinking most easily when it is organized as a pyramid under one main point. Supporting arguments sit beneath it, and each point summarizes the ones below it [1]. Nielsen Norman Group's guidance for web writing reaches the same conclusion from how people actually read: readers scan and may stop at any moment, so the most important information, "even…the conclusion," belongs first [2]. Engineering leader Will Larson notes that executives who already agree can stop reading after the first line, and that's a feature [3].
*Nonprofit example:* Don't open with "Over the past quarter we explored several intake options…" Open with "I recommend we move volunteer intake to the online form by March. It cuts sign-up time from about two weeks to two days, costs $40/month, and needs a yes from you by Friday." Then give three reasons, then the details.

**2. Make the main point a sentence, not a topic.** Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, author of the bestselling *Storytelling with Data*, borrows Nancy Duarte's "Big Idea": one complete sentence that states your point of view and what's at stake [4]. She pairs it with the "3-minute story", which tests whether you could explain your message with no slides at all [5]. "Volunteer intake update" is a topic. "Slow intake is costing us about one in three volunteers who sign up, and a $40 tool can fix most of it" is a Big Idea. If you can't write that sentence, you aren't ready to present.

**3. Use a short written memo for important decisions, not a deck.** Jeff Bezos's 2017 shareholder letter describes Amazon's practice of replacing slide decks with roughly six-page narrative memos that everyone reads silently at the start of the meeting. He stresses that great memos are drafted, reviewed by colleagues, and rewritten, and that a great memo "probably should take a week or more" [6]. You don't have a week or six pages to spare, and your board doesn't either. Borrow the principle, not the length. Write a **one-page memo** with your recommendation, the problem, the options you considered, cost and risk, and the specific decision needed. Send it with the board packet. BoardSource recommends sending agendas and attachments at least a week before the meeting and framing agenda items as questions for the board [7].

**4. Pair one real story with one honest number, and know which does what.** A meta-analysis in *Health Communication* found that statistical evidence moved **beliefs and attitudes** more, while narrative evidence moved **intentions** more. The authors advise matching the evidence type to your objective [8]. Research on "narrative transportation" shows that people who become absorbed in a story are more persuaded by it [9]. The Heaths' *Made to Stick* likewise argues that concrete, emotional stories make ideas memorable [10]. With leadership you usually need both: they must *believe* the problem is real (number) and *want to act* (story).
*Example:* "Last month Maria, a home health aide, tried three times to book a food pantry slot on her phone and gave up (shared with her permission). She isn't alone: 38% of mobile booking attempts were abandoned." Use real numbers from your own data, never invented ones.

**5. Don't oversell the "one story beats statistics" idea.** Nonprofit training often cites Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (2007). In their study, people gave more to a single identified child than to a statistical description, and adding statistics to the child's story *reduced* giving [11]. But a 2016 meta-analysis of 41 effects found the overall identifiable-victim effect was small (r = .05) and depended on conditions like a single victim, a photo, and a monetary request [12]. A pre-registered replication of the 2007 study found no support for the effect [13]. Another replication of a related classic study also failed, and a re-analysis of the meta-analysis found signs of publication bias [14]. The practical lesson for you: a story is a doorway into the data, not a substitute for it. Board decisions *should* be deliberative. Treat the story as a reason to look closely, then show the evidence.

**6. Tell stories ethically.** You hold power over the people whose stories you tell. Blue Avocado's guidance: get specific, informed consent for each use (a blanket release isn't enough). Show people through their strengths rather than as "saved". Let them review drafts. Never trade on trauma [15]. In an internal memo, anonymize by default ("a parent in our evening program") unless you have consent to use a name.

**7. Make your work visible with a short, regular update.** Teresa Torres (Product Talk) warns against "saving everything for a big reveal." Stakeholders who hear nothing for weeks form their own conclusions. She recommends sharing outcomes, what you learned from users, and your test results early and often [16]. Lenny Rachitsky's widely used "State of Me" template keeps a regular update to your manager to three parts: blockers and the help you need, current priorities with progress, and what's on your mind [17].
*For you:* a five-bullet email to your ED every other Friday covers what shipped, what you learned from users, one number, one blocker, and what's next. Once a quarter, condense it into a half-page for the board packet. It also builds a written record you can draw on for grant reports.

**8. Pre-wire decisions in the open.** Pre-wiring means walking key decision-makers through a proposal one-on-one before the formal meeting. Venture investor Mark Suster advises calling each board member about a week ahead. Preview the pros, cons, and costs, ask directly where they stand and what worries them, then confirm decisions in writing afterward [18]. Teresa Torres frames these conversations as sharing your work and inviting input, not selling conclusions [16]. Practitioners also name the downsides: it takes time, too much input can water down the proposal, and in low-trust settings it gives opponents a head start [19]. In a nonprofit, keep it transparent. Tell your ED you're doing it, give every board member the same information, and use the conversations to *improve* the proposal, not to count votes behind anyone's back.
*Example:* before the board meets on a donor database switch, spend 20 minutes with the treasurer on cost, 15 with the program director on staff training time, and 10 with the ED on timing. Then revise the memo so it answers their concerns.

**9. Tailor to the reader's question.** The Heaths' "curse of knowledge" describes how experts forget what it's like not to know. Leaders who are steeped in their own logic state strategy in abstractions that others can't act on [20]. The same applies to you: "API integration" means nothing to a retired-teacher board member. Before you write anything, ask what *this* reader needs to decide or worry about. For the ED, that's usually risk, staff capacity, and funders. For the treasurer, cost. For program staff, workload and clients. For the board as a whole, mission and fiduciary risk. Same Big Idea, different supporting points first.

## Try this in the next two weeks

- [ ] Rewrite your next email to your ED so the first sentence is the recommendation or the ask.
- [ ] Write one Big Idea sentence for your most important current project, and test it aloud as a 3-minute story with a colleague.
- [ ] Pick one decision you need and draft a one-page memo: recommendation, problem, options, cost/risk, decision needed.
- [ ] Find one number from your own data and one consented (or anonymized) beneficiary story that illustrate the same problem.
- [ ] Send your first five-bullet biweekly update and put the next one on your calendar.
- [ ] Before the next board or leadership meeting, hold two or three short one-on-one previews. Tell your ED you're doing it.

## Where this advice breaks down

- **Answer-first can backfire with skeptical audiences.** Most support for the pyramid comes from consulting practice and expert opinion, not controlled studies. Even its fans note it is aimed at readers open to the conclusion [3]. If your ED is strongly attached to the current approach, spend a sentence or two on the shared problem before the recommendation. The structure stays the same, only the opening changes.
- **Amazon's memo culture doesn't transfer whole.** A week per memo [6] is unrealistic for a solo PM, and volunteer boards may not read a six-pager. Shrink the format, keep the discipline.
- **Story-vs-statistics research is mostly about donations and health messages,** studied with students and online panels [8][11][13]. It isn't about board decisions. Treat it as a reason to use both kinds of evidence, not as a formula. The identifiable victim effect in particular is contested [12][13][14].
- **Pre-wiring can tip into politics.** Suster's advice comes from venture-backed company boards, where the CEO counts votes [18]. Nonprofit boards have a duty to deliberate together, so private lobbying that leaves some members out of the loop can erode trust and governance. Keep previews informational and even-handed [7][19].
- **Regular updates can become noise.** If nobody reads them, shorten them or ask your ED what format they'd actually use.

```text
I'm the only product manager at a small nonprofit (no PM team, few staff, limited budget). I report to an Executive Director, we have a volunteer board, and we depend on donors and funders. I just read an article on "Communicating for influence: clear writing, storytelling with data, and presenting to leadership and the board." Please act as my study coach.

Key ideas from the article:
1. Answer first (Minto Pyramid Principle / bottom line up front): open with the recommendation or ask, then 3 or so supporting reasons, then details. Caveat: with skeptical readers, briefly establish the shared problem first.
2. Write the main point as a "Big Idea": one complete sentence with your point of view and what's at stake (Knaflic, after Duarte). Test it with a "3-minute story" without slides.
3. For important decisions, use a one-page written memo (adapted from Amazon's six-page narratives): recommendation, problem, options, cost/risk, decision needed. Send board materials at least a week ahead and frame items as questions (BoardSource).
4. Pair one real, consented story with one honest number. A meta-analysis found statistics shift beliefs and attitudes more, while narratives shift intentions more. Use both.
5. The "identifiable victim effect" (Small, Loewenstein & Slovic 2007) is contested: a meta-analysis found a small effect (r = .05), and pre-registered replications failed. A story is a doorway into the data, not a substitute.
6. Tell stories ethically: specific informed consent for each use, strengths-based framing, let people review drafts, anonymize by default internally.
7. Make work visible with short regular updates (e.g., a 5-bullet biweekly email: shipped, learned, one number, one blocker, next). Avoid the "big reveal."
8. Pre-wire decisions with 1:1 previews before meetings, but transparently: tell the ED, give all board members the same information, and use the talks to improve the proposal, not to lobby in secret. Downsides: time, dilution, sabotage risk.
9. Tailor to the reader (curse of knowledge): ED = risk, capacity, funders; treasurer = cost; program staff = workload and clients; board = mission and fiduciary risk.

How to quiz me:
- Ask ONE question at a time and wait for my answer before continuing.
- Mix three types: recall ("What are the parts of a one-page decision memo?"), application to a scenario ("Rewrite this opening line answer-first"), and "what would you do" judgment calls.
- After each answer, give feedback: what I got right, what I missed, then a model answer with your reasoning.
- Adapt difficulty: harder if I answer well, simpler with hints if I struggle.
- Include at least one multi-person scenario involving an Executive Director, a board member, and a program staff member (for example: proposing a new client intake tool where the ED worries about funder reporting, a board treasurer worries about cost, and a program coordinator worries about extra workload). Ask me how I'd sequence pre-wiring conversations, tailor the message, and structure the memo.
- Include at least one question that tests whether I understand the limits of the evidence (e.g., the identifiable victim effect replications, or why Amazon's memo practice must be scaled down).
- If you are unsure about a fact, research finding, or source, say so plainly rather than inventing details, statistics, or citations.

After about 8 to 10 questions, end with: (a) a short summary of my strengths, (b) my main gaps, and (c) three specific follow-up actions I can take at my nonprofit in the next two weeks.
```
