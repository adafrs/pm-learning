# Sustaining Yourself and Your Influence as a Team of One

> **In one paragraph:** Burnout is common in nonprofits. Research says it comes mostly from the job itself: too much work, too little control, unclear roles and values that clash. It is not mainly a personal weakness. As the only PM, you are at higher risk because everyone's requests land on you and few people understand what your role is. Five things help. Write down what the PM role does and doesn't do. Protect a block of focused time. Give help in ways you can keep up ("otherish" giving rather than selfless giving). Build a few internal champions and a loose outside network of other PMs. Teach others basic product skills so your influence doesn't depend on your energy alone. None of this replaces fixing workload problems with your Executive Director, but it makes those conversations easier and your work more durable.

## Why this matters when you're the only PM

Nonprofit leaders are worried about burnout. In the Center for Effective Philanthropy's 2024 survey of 239 U.S. nonprofit leaders, 95% had some concern about staff burnout. A quarter said it was moderately or significantly hurting their ability to deliver on the mission. Half were more worried about their own burnout than a year before [1].

The World Health Organization defines burnout as a syndrome caused by *chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed*. It has three parts: exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about the job, and a reduced sense of effectiveness. WHO calls it an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition [2]. Maslach and Leiter's long research program reaches the same conclusion: burnout is "job-related and situation-specific" [3]. It grows when people are mismatched with their jobs in six areas: workload, control, reward, community, fairness and values [3].

As a team of one, you face several of those mismatches at once:

- **Workload.** Every department's requests come to you.
- **Control.** You lead through influence, not authority.
- **Community.** You have no peer down the hall who does the same job.
- **Values.** You may feel guilty saying no to anything that serves clients.

A meta-analysis of burnout's correlates found that job *demands* are linked to emotional exhaustion more strongly than job *resources* are [4]. So cutting and clarifying demands is as important as adding support.

Your influence is also at stake. A burned-out PM becomes cynical and less effective, which are two of WHO's three dimensions [2]. Colleagues stop listening to someone who looks overwhelmed.

## Practical advice

1. **Make the role legible: write a one-page "What the PM does and doesn't do."** Burnout research links unclear and excessive demands to exhaustion [3][4]. Practitioners note that the PM role is often misunderstood, and they describe it in terms of outcomes and decisions rather than every task nearby [11]. Draft a page with three columns: *I own* (e.g., deciding what the client intake app does next, running user interviews with program participants), *I support* (e.g., grant reports that need product data), and *I don't do* (e.g., fixing the office printer, being the default IT help desk, building every donor spreadsheet). Agree on it with your Executive Director and share it at a staff meeting. Then you can point to it when you need to say no.

2. **Protect a focused block for real product work.** Marty Cagan of SVPG says PMs at tech companies need "on the order of four solid hours a day" for discovery work, and that they should hand project coordination to someone else where they can [10]. Four hours is unrealistic in most small nonprofits. The principle still holds: if you don't defend product time, meetings and coordination will eat it. Start with two half-day blocks a week on your calendar, and tell your ED what they are for (for example, "client interviews and roadmap thinking").

3. **Give help in a way you can keep up.** Adam Grant's research separates *selfless* givers, who are most exposed to burnout and exploitation, from *otherish* givers. Otherish givers care about others but also protect their own goals, so they can keep giving for longer [8]. Grant also recommends "five-minute favors": help that costs you little but is valuable to the other person [8]. Grant, Cross and Rebele report that time spent on collaborative work has grown by 50% or more in recent decades [9]. Grant and Rebele's HBR piece argues that giving without limits leads to exhaustion and can hurt the people you meant to help [9]. In practice: hold weekly "product office hours" instead of answering ad-hoc requests all day, batch small requests, and when you say yes, ask what you can drop.

4. **Name the culture openly. Don't silently absorb it.** SSIR writers argue that nonprofit leaders, boards and colleagues reinforce overwork by treating everything as urgent [6]. They also argue that leaders need to model rest, for example by not sending late-night emails, because staff copy what leaders do [6][7]. You can't fix this alone. You can bring it to your ED as a *mission* question: "If I keep taking every request, the intake redesign we promised the funder slips. Which two of these should wait?" The goal is to move the problem from your stamina to a shared priority decision.

5. **Build three or four internal champions on purpose.** Influence without authority depends on relationships, and the six-areas model counts *community* as a burnout protector [3]. Pick one program staff member who sees clients every day, one finance or development person who talks to funders, and, if possible, one board member with relevant skills. Meet each one briefly every month. Share early drafts with them and give them credit in public. When your roadmap reaches the ED or board, they will speak up for it, and you won't be carrying it alone.

6. **Build a loose outside network, not only close friends.** Granovetter's classic paper argued that weak ties (acquaintances, former colleagues) act as bridges that bring new information your close circle doesn't have [12]. A 2022 randomized study of more than 20 million LinkedIn members confirmed a causal benefit of weaker ties for job mobility. It also found that *moderately* weak ties helped most [13]. For you, that means a modest number of casual connections in different circles. Places to find them:
   - NTEN's free online groups and local tech clubs, which are open to nonprofit staff, volunteers and board members [15].
   - Mind the Product's Slack, which has more than 60,000 product people and an #advice channel, and its free local ProductTank meetups [16].
   - Fast Forward, if your organization is itself a tech nonprofit [17].

   Aim to show up and help a little at a time rather than networking hard.

7. **Find a mentor, then become one.** A meta-analysis in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* found that mentored people had better career outcomes than those without mentors. The effects on career satisfaction were clear, while effects on objective outcomes like pay were small [14]. A separate meta-analysis found that *mentors* also reported higher job satisfaction and organizational commitment than non-mentors [18]. Ask an experienced PM from your outside network for one call a month. Inside your organization, coach one or two colleagues in basic product skills, such as running a five-person client interview or writing a problem statement. That builds capacity that stays when you are on leave or after you move on, and it spreads product thinking without you being in every room.

8. **Learn in public, and keep it small.** Share a short monthly "what we learned from users" note with staff and board. Post an occasional anonymized lesson in a peer community. This makes your work visible, which supports the legibility point above. It invites help from weak ties [12]. It also turns your learning into something others can reuse. Keep client data out of it and get your ED's blessing first.

- [ ] Block two half-days on your calendar for product work and label what they're for.
- [ ] Announce one weekly "product office hours" slot and redirect ad-hoc requests to it.
- [ ] List your four potential champions (program, finance/development, board, one wildcard) and book a 20-minute conversation with one of them.
- [ ] Join one outside community (an NTEN online group or the Mind the Product Slack) and answer one question there.
- [ ] Choose one colleague and teach them one product skill this month.
- [ ] Notice your own warning signs (exhaustion, cynicism, feeling ineffective) [2] and name one to someone you trust.

## Where this advice breaks down

- **Individual tactics can't fix a structurally overloaded job.** Maslach and Leiter note that most interventions target individuals, even though the evidence points to situational causes, and that few interventions have been rigorously evaluated [3]. If your role is really two jobs, boundaries alone won't save you. That calls for a workload and staffing conversation with your ED, and possibly with funders. CEP's data point to sector-wide pressures, including pay and capacity, that one PM can't solve [1][7].
- **The definition of burnout is disputed.** A 14-sample meta-analysis found that exhaustion, burnout's core, correlates very strongly with depression. The authors argue burnout may not be a distinct syndrome [5]. Practical point: if you feel persistently low, don't treat it as "just burnout" to fix with time management. Talk to a health professional.
- **Weak-tie findings come from tech labor markets.** The LinkedIn study found weak ties mattered more in digital industries, while *strong* ties mattered more in less digital ones [13]. In tight local nonprofit ecosystems, a few trusted relationships may matter more than a broad network.
- **Mentoring effects are modest.** Benefits for objective outcomes were small [14]. Mentoring studies are mostly correlational, and the mentor-benefit review called for longitudinal research [18]. Treat mentoring as helpful, not transformative.
- **Big-tech PM advice doesn't transfer one-for-one.** Cagan's four-hour discovery block assumes a delivery team and a delivery manager [10]. In a five-person nonprofit you may also be the project manager, so scale the principle down rather than dropping it.
- **"Otherish" evidence is popular science.** Grant's giver/taker framework comes from a bestselling book and his research summaries [8][9]. The specific "otherish" stamina claim is less directly tested than the burnout research. Treat it as a useful lens.
