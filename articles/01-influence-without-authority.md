# Influence Without Authority for the Solo Nonprofit PM

> **In one paragraph:** As the only product manager at a small nonprofit, you manage almost no one, yet almost everything you ship depends on other people: program staff, the development team, volunteers, your Executive Director (ED) and sometimes the board. The most useful working model is exchange: work out what each person cares about (their "currencies") and offer something they value in return for what you need. Research on influence tactics says to lead with well-evidenced reasoning, real consultation, and appeals to the mission. Pressure and going over people's heads tend to get grudging compliance at best. Your currency is credibility, earned by doing what you say, bringing evidence instead of opinions, and helping people before you need them. Coalitions matter, but in a small organization they are built one sincere conversation at a time, not through politics.

## Why this matters when you're the only PM

In most organizations, product managers have little direct authority over the things that make their products succeed [10]. In a small nonprofit this is sharper. You have no PM peers to back you up. The program director owns the client relationship, the development director owns the funder relationship, and the ED owns the final call. Many of the people whose help you need are volunteers or part-time contractors who can simply stop showing up.

That means your influence has to come from somewhere other than your title. The research gives a reasonably clear picture of what works, and it is mostly good news for mission-driven people. The tactics that build real commitment are the honest ones: explaining your reasoning, asking for input you will actually use, and connecting the work to why everyone is there [3][4]. Manipulation tends to backfire over time, because people stop trusting someone they see as calculating [1].

## Practical advice

1. **Assume everyone is a potential ally, then diagnose their world before you ask.** Cohen and Bradford's model starts with treating the other person as a potential partner, clarifying what you actually need, and understanding the pressures that shape their priorities, such as how they are measured and what they answer for [1][2]. Lenny's Newsletter guidance for PMs says the same: hold informal one-on-ones to learn each stakeholder's goals, competing projects, decision process and likely objections before you pitch [11]. *Nonprofit example:* before asking the case-management lead to pilot a new intake form, learn that she is judged on how many clients her team sees each week and that a funder report is due next month. Your "ask" now has to save her time, not cost it.

2. **Map currencies, including the ones you already hold.** Cohen and Bradford group what people value into inspiration-related currencies (vision, excellence, doing what's right), task-related ones (resources, help, information, rapid response), and position-, relationship- and personal-related ones [1][2]. Their key point is that people underestimate what they can offer without budget or permission: recognition, appreciation, useful information, help with an unwanted task [1]. *Nonprofit example:* the development director may value a clean outcomes chart for the spring appeal far more than a new feature. You can give that; it is an hour of your time.

3. **Lead with rational persuasion, consultation and the mission.** In Yukl and Tracey's field study of managers (cited more than 800 times), rational persuasion, inspirational appeals and consultation were rated most effective. Pressure, coalition (ganging up) and appeals to authority were rated least effective [3]. A later meta-analysis of 49 samples found rational persuasion was the only tactic with consistently positive links to both task and relationship outcomes, while pressure was negatively related [4]. *Nonprofit example:* "Here is what 12 clients told us about the sign-up form, here is the drop-off data, and I'd like your view on which fix to try first" combines all three: evidence, a real request for input, and a link to client outcomes.

4. **With your ED and board, rely on evidence, not favors or flattery.** Yukl and Tracey found that ingratiation and exchange worked moderately with peers and subordinates but not with superiors, and rational persuasion was the tactic most often used upward [3]. Save trades for peers. Upward, bring a short, well-reasoned case.

5. **Build credibility by turning opinions into small tests.** Marty Cagan of SVPG argues that a PM's credibility rests on deep knowledge of users, data, technology and the organization, shared openly. When opinions clash, he recommends running a quick test to gather evidence, because in a battle of opinions the most senior person tends to win [10]. Cohen and Bradford note that trust shapes every exchange and cannot be faked at the last minute [1]. *Nonprofit example:* instead of debating whether volunteers will use text reminders, send them to one shift's volunteers for two weeks and report what happened.

6. **Give first, sincerely, and expect it to come back slowly.** Reciprocity is the foundation of the exchange model [1] and one of Cialdini's core principles of influence [5][6]. The Influence at Work summary of Cialdini's work says giving first, and making it personal and unexpected, works best [6]. A 2021 direct replication of Cialdini's classic reciprocity experiment found similar compliance rates 45 years later [7]. *Nonprofit example:* SSIR contributor Jenka Soderberg describes gaining influence at a community radio station by reorganizing volunteer training (outside her job) and sending volunteers to help an overwhelmed development director [12]. Staff in stretched nonprofits often won't ask for help, so offering it builds goodwill [12].

7. **Preview ideas one-on-one before group meetings.** Cagan recommends regular individual time with key stakeholders and showing them proposed solutions before they are built, not after [10]. Large group meetings tend to turn into design by committee [10]. Walter calls this priming detractors and champions in "the meeting before the meeting" [11]. *Nonprofit example:* show your ED and the program director the draft roadmap separately before the staff meeting, so their concerns shape it and no one is surprised in public. Cagan suggests 3–4 hours a week [10]; a solo PM might scale that down to a 20-minute check-in with each of your four or five key people.

8. **Collaborate rather than compromise.** Cagan argues that collaboration is not consensus, voting, or splitting the difference, which tends to produce mediocre results. It means combining each person's expertise toward a solution that is valuable, usable, feasible and viable [9]. In a nonprofit, add "consistent with our mission and safe for clients."

9. **Build a network, not a faction.** Battilana and Casciaro studied 68 change initiatives in the UK's National Health Service. They found that change agents who were central in the informal network, or who bridged disconnected groups, were more effective, whatever their formal rank [8]. Strong personal ties to influential people who were undecided about a change helped win them over. Strong ties to firm opponents helped only when the change was close to existing practice and hurt for radical change [8]. *Nonprofit example:* if you are moving case notes from paper to a database, spend time with the respected senior caseworker who is on the fence. Don't pin everything on converting the loudest opponent.

10. **Keep hard tactics as a last resort, and name your intent.** Cohen and Bradford accept that you may sometimes need to state consequences ("without the data export, the funder report will slip"). They warn that opening with threats out of frustration creates escalating resistance [1]. The research also shows pressure is linked to worse outcomes [4]. Escalating to the ED is legitimate when work is truly blocked. Say so openly to the colleague first.

## Try this in the next two weeks

- [ ] List the 5–7 people whose cooperation your product most depends on (include your ED, one board member, one program lead, one volunteer leader).
- [ ] For each, write one line on what they are measured on or worried about, and two currencies you could offer them.
- [ ] Hold one 20-minute listening conversation with someone you've been treating as an obstacle. Ask, don't pitch.
- [ ] Do one unrequested, useful favor (a data pull, a fixed spreadsheet, an hour of help) with no ask attached.
- [ ] Turn one current disagreement into a small, time-boxed test with an agreed measure.
- [ ] Before your next staff or board meeting, preview your proposal one-on-one with the two people most affected.
- [ ] Rewrite your next upward request as: the problem, the evidence, the options, your recommendation, and what you need from them.

## Where this advice breaks down

- **The evidence is correlational and mostly corporate.** Yukl and Tracey relied on questionnaires about managers [3]. The meta-analysis found that effects for most tactics depend on moderators, with only rational persuasion consistently positive [4]. Neither focused on small nonprofits, volunteers, or boards.
- **"Rational persuasion" only works with genuinely strong reasons.** Weak data dressed up as evidence will cost you credibility, not build it [10].
- **Exchange language can feel wrong in a mission culture.** Soderberg, writing in SSIR, pushes back on the idea that exchange drives all relationships. She argues that much nonprofit giving is motivated by care rather than expectation of return [12]. Cohen and Bradford themselves warn that being seen as a tit-for-tat "wheeler-dealer" loses influence [1]. Treat currencies as a way to understand people, not a ledger to collect on.
- **Network findings come from a very large system.** The NHS study involved large organizations with many groups to bridge [8]. In a 10-person nonprofit the informal network may be the whole staff. The ED's view can outweigh any coalition, so a strong one-on-one relationship with the ED matters more.
- **Persuasion science is partly lab-based.** Cialdini's reciprocity finding replicated well in a direct replication, but that is one study of one effect [7]. Don't overgeneralize other "persuasion hacks." Never invent scarcity or social proof (for example "everyone else has agreed" when they haven't). It is unethical and, once found out, destroys trust [1][6].
- **Influence is not a substitute for clear decision rights.** If no one knows who decides product priorities, influence tactics will only get you so far. That is a governance conversation to have with your ED.
- **Time is finite.** SVPG's 3–4 hours a week assumes a dedicated product team [10]. Scale it to what a solo role can hold.
