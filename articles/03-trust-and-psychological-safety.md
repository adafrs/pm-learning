# Trust and Psychological Safety as the Source of Lasting Influence

> **In one paragraph:** As the only product manager at a small nonprofit, you rarely have formal authority. You get influence from being trusted. Research points to two things you can control. First, be someone whose words and actions match: keep promises, be honest about what you know, and put the mission ahead of your own credit. Second, help create a team where people can safely raise problems, admit mistakes, and disagree. Neither one means being "nice." Both work best alongside clear standards and shared accountability. The practical version is small and repeatable: track your commitments, admit your own mistakes first, ask before you tell, and run short blameless reviews when things go wrong.

## Why this matters when you're the only PM

You can't make the program director use the new intake form, make the Executive Director (ED) delay a launch, or make a board member drop a pet feature. What you can do is become the person whose read on a problem others act on. That is earned trust, and you earn it through behavior that people see repeatedly.

Small nonprofits make this both easier and harder. It's easier because everyone sees what you do, so a kept promise is noticed quickly. It's harder because staff are stretched, many are deeply committed to the mission, and pointing out a problem can feel like criticizing a colleague's dedication or an ED's vision. Volunteers and frontline staff often notice a broken workflow first and say so last. If they don't feel safe telling you, you build the wrong thing. Research on team learning suggests this pattern is common (people stay quiet when speaking up feels risky), and a product role depends on hearing the bad news early [1][5].

## Practical advice

**1. Treat "doing what you said" as your main source of credibility.** Tony Simons calls the perceived match between a manager's words and deeds "behavioral integrity," and it covers both keeping promises and acting on the values you state [8]. A later meta-analysis found that behavioral integrity has stronger links to trust, task performance, and helpful extra-role behavior than general "moral integrity" does [9]. The trust equation in *The Trusted Advisor* makes the same point in practitioner terms: trust rises with credibility, reliability, and intimacy, and falls as self-orientation rises [7]. *In practice:* keep a visible commitments list. When you tell the development director "I'll have the donor-data fix scoped by Thursday," it goes on the list. If you'll miss it, say so before Thursday, not after. Make fewer promises and keep all of them.

**2. Lower your "self-orientation" in a mission-driven way.** In the trust equation, self-orientation sits in the denominator, so it divides everything else [7]. At a nonprofit, the easiest way to show low self-orientation is to frame every product request around beneficiaries, not around your roadmap. Give credit publicly to the case manager whose idea the feature came from. When you disagree with the ED, open with the outcome you both want ("fewer families dropping out between referral and first visit").

**3. Know what psychological safety is, and what it isn't.** Edmondson defined it as a shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. In her original study of 51 teams, it predicted learning behavior, and learning behavior in turn predicted performance [1]. Google's Project Aristotle, a study of 180 internal teams, ranked it first among the five dynamics of effective teams, ahead of dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact [6]. Edmondson is explicit that it is not an "anything goes" culture, not about comfort, and not a replacement for high standards [3]. Her 2025 HBR article with Kerrissey lists common misreadings: that it means avoiding conflict, endorsing every idea, or removing accountability [4]. *In practice:* you can be warm and still say "this pilot didn't hit the target we set; let's figure out why."

**4. Go first on admitting mistakes.** Edmondson's recommended leader moves include acknowledging your own fallibility, framing the work as learning, asking real questions, and thanking people who bring bad news [3]. A 2023 review of 185 papers by Edmondson and Bransby, summarized by Harvard Business School, highlights normalizing learning from mistakes and seeking input with humility [5]. *In practice:* at the next staff meeting, name one thing you got wrong ("I underestimated how long the volunteer-scheduling migration would take, and that's on me. Here's what I'm changing."). Status matters here. When the person with the most information about the product admits error, others can too.

**5. Ask before you tell, especially across power gaps.** The Nonprofit Risk Management Center notes that formal authority filters what leaders hear. It recommends hearing everyone's views before sharing your own, building on ideas with "and," and not getting attached to your own proposals [13]. Edmondson makes the same recommendation (model curiosity, create forums for input) [3]. *In practice:* in a feature-prioritization meeting with program staff, ask the quietest frontline person first. With the board, bring a question ("What would make you confident this is worth funding?") before you bring a recommendation.

**6. Run blameless reviews, scaled down.** Google's site-reliability practice defines a blameless postmortem as one that looks for contributing causes without indicting any person. It assumes people acted in good faith with the information they had, because fear of blame leads people to hide incidents. The authors put it as "You can't 'fix' people, but you can fix systems and processes" [10]. Edmondson's research supports treating failures as learning opportunities while still addressing clear violations [3]. *In practice:* after a donation-form outage on a giving day, or a wrong-language reminder text to clients, hold a 30-minute review. Ask four questions: what happened, what we knew at the time, what made it easy to go wrong, and what one or two changes we'll make. Write it up in one page, assign owners, and follow up. Skip anything that turns into "who."

**7. Give candid feedback, but lead with care and invite it back.** Kim Scott's Radical Candor framework pairs "care personally" with "challenge directly." She argues that most management failures are "ruinous empathy," meaning care without challenge. She advises giving criticism in private, keeping it specific, and checking how your feedback landed [11]. In a mission-driven culture where people avoid conflict, this is a useful corrective. But Scott herself warns that people misuse the label to justify harshness [12]. Start by asking for criticism of your own work before you hand out much of your own.

**8. Extend transparency outward, carefully.** Some nonprofits have found that being open about failure can strengthen funder relationships. Engineers Without Borders Canada has published annual failure reports since 2008 [14]. The Chronicle of Philanthropy has argued that hiding mistakes encourages donors to believe every project succeeds [15]. The same SSIR article notes that admitting failure feels risky to organizations that depend heavily on funders [14]. *In practice:* agree with your ED on what gets shared and with whom before you tell a funder about a product misstep.

**9. Keep safety and accountability together.** Pair every "it's safe to raise this" with "and here's the bar we agreed on." The research summarized under "Where this advice breaks down" suggests that safety without shared accountability can let performance drift [16][17].

## Try this in the next two weeks

- [ ] Start a commitments list (date, promise, person). Review it every Friday and send a heads-up for anything at risk.
- [ ] In one meeting, name a mistake you made and the change you're making because of it.
- [ ] In your next prioritization or design conversation, ask a frontline staff member or volunteer for their view before you give yours.
- [ ] Write a one-page blameless review template (what happened, what we knew, contributing conditions, changes, owners) and use it on the next hiccup, however small.
- [ ] Ask your ED one question: "What's one thing I could do differently that would make my recommendations easier for you to act on?"
- [ ] Rewrite one upcoming request so it opens with the beneficiary outcome rather than the feature.

## Where this advice breaks down

- **Most of the evidence is correlational.** The 2017 meta-analysis (136 samples, more than 22,000 people) supports psychological safety as an established construct, but a widely used evidence summary notes that most included studies were cross-sectional and many effects were moderate, so causation is not proven [2][18]. Edmondson's founding study looked at 51 teams in one manufacturing company [1]. Project Aristotle was an internal study at one large tech company and was not peer-reviewed [6]. None of these were done in five-person nonprofits.
- **More is not always better.** Cappelli, Eldor, and Hodor report that moderate psychological safety was associated with better performance, while very high levels were associated with declining performance. They argue maximizing it "should have little payoff" in routine jobs and propose collective accountability as the remedy [16]. Deng and colleagues propose a "dark" pathway in which a comfortable climate lowers motivation, although critics point out that the study did not measure accountability directly [17]. *Implication:* don't chase comfort. Aim for safety to speak up within clear expectations.
- **You can't install it alone or by policy.** Edmondson and Kerrissey argue that it is built "interaction by interaction" and can't be mandated, although any team at any level can start [4]. If your ED punishes bad news, your efforts will have limited reach. Say so privately, and protect the team space you do control.
- **The neuroscience is weaker than it sounds.** Paul Zak's popular HBR article ties eight trust-building management behaviors (recognize excellence, share information broadly, show vulnerability, and others) to oxytocin, and it reports large gaps between high- and low-trust companies in self-reported survey data [19]. A critical review by Nave, Camerer, and McCullough concluded that the oxytocin-trust link has not replicated robustly [20]. The behaviors are still sensible, but don't cite the brain chemistry to your board.
- **Candor frameworks get misapplied.** Even the framework's author documents Radical Candor being used as cover for being a jerk [12]. In small teams with long relationships and volunteers who can simply leave, harsh feedback is expensive. Err toward private, specific, and kind.
- **Funder transparency has real risk.** Openness about failure helped some organizations but feels risky to heavily funder-dependent ones [14]. Decide on external disclosure with your ED.
