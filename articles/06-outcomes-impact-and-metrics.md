# Connecting Product Work to Mission Outcomes

> **In one paragraph:** Your board and funders already think in theories of change and logic models: inputs lead to activities, activities produce outputs, and outputs are supposed to lead to outcomes for the people you serve. If you describe your product work in that same chain, your roadmap stops looking like "IT stuff" and starts looking like mission work. The skill is choosing a few outcome measures that are credible, that you will actually use, and that your small team can afford to collect, while guarding against the well-documented tendency of any number that matters to get gamed. You do not need a randomized trial. You need a clear story of how your product helps, two or three honest measures, and a habit of reviewing them.

## Why this matters when you're the only PM

In a small nonprofit, nobody hands you a metrics team or an analytics platform. You also face a culture where "we helped 1,200 people" is the default way to talk about success. That is an output. It counts activity, not change in anyone's life. The W.K. Kellogg Foundation's widely used guide puts it plainly: "Outputs are data about activities" [1]. Outcomes are changes in participants' knowledge, behavior, skills, or status [1].

Product management has the same trap. Melissa Perri calls it the "build trap": teams measure success by features shipped rather than by what changed for users [12][13]. As the only PM, you can become the person who connects your shipping to mission outcomes. That is a positive, ethical form of influence: you are helping the Executive Director (ED), board, and funders see what is working, in the language they already use.

## Practical advice

1. **Learn the vocabulary your funders use, and map your product onto it.** A logic model links resources (inputs), activities, outputs, short-term and long-term outcomes, and impact [1]. A theory of change goes further. It works backwards from a long-term goal to the preconditions needed to reach it and spells out the assumptions behind each link [2]. Most funders ask for one or both. Before you propose new metrics, find your organization's existing logic model, often buried in a grant application, and add a "product" row. For example: a text-reminder feature (activity) leads to reminders sent (output), which leads to fewer missed appointments (short-term outcome), which leads to stable housing applications completed (longer-term outcome). Now your feature lives inside the story the board already approved [1][2].

2. **Separate outputs from outcomes in every report, and keep both.** Outputs are not useless. They tell you whether the program was delivered at the intended "dose" [1]. But don't present them as success. Ebrahim and Rangan argue that operating organizations, especially smaller ones with narrow scope, are often better off measuring outputs and individual-level outcomes they can actually attribute. They argue long-term societal impact is better measured by funders [19]. So a realistic target for you is: "outputs we control, plus one or two near-term outcomes for the people we serve." Don't claim population-level impact you can't support.

3. **Right-size measurement with the CART test.** Gugerty and Karlan's CART principles are a practical filter for every metric you propose [3][4]:
   - **Credible:** Is the data valid and reliable, and analyzed properly?
   - **Actionable:** Will we change something based on it? If not, don't collect it.
   - **Responsible:** Do the benefits outweigh the costs, including the time burden on clients and staff?
   - **Transportable:** Does it teach us, or others, something reusable?

   Their SSIR article lists ten situations where a formal impact evaluation is the wrong choice. Examples include an untested theory of change, unstable implementation, and too little money to get a conclusive answer [3]. Most small-nonprofit product work falls into at least one of these. The World Bank's David Evans, reviewing their book, makes the same point: nearly every program needs good monitoring, but not every program needs an impact evaluation [4]. Use this to reassure an anxious ED. Good monitoring is a legitimate, respected choice, not a cop-out.

4. **Pick two or three measures, not twenty.** The Leap of Reason Ambassadors Community's *Performance Imperative* is a framework many funders recognize. It asks the board, management, and staff to agree jointly on indicators "tightly aligned" with the intended results [5]. It also says no organization should try to tackle all seven of its pillars at once [5][6]. Its version for small nonprofits says to start with one area and celebrate incremental progress [6]. In practice, choose one outcome measure, one or two outputs that predict it, and one qualitative signal such as client feedback. The Performance Imperative explicitly calls for collecting constituent feedback [5].

5. **Pair every key number with a guardrail number.** Campbell's law, from a peer-reviewed 1979 paper cited over 1,100 times, warns that the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressures [7][8]. Goodhart's law describes the same failure: pushing hard on a proxy breaks its link to the real goal [9]. Perri recommends a system of balanced metrics rather than a single one, because a single metric is easy to game [13]. Nonprofit examples:
   - "Applications submitted" paired with "applications approved."
   - "Volunteers onboarded" paired with "volunteers still active at 90 days."
   - "Time to intake" paired with "client-reported satisfaction with intake."

6. **Use OKRs lightly, if at all, and never tie them to pay or grant penalties.** John Doerr's *Measure What Matters*, a #1 New York Times bestseller, popularized OKRs: an ambitious objective with a few measurable key results [10]. The method's own materials recommend about three to five key results per objective. They also say OKRs should be kept separate from compensation [11]. For a solo PM, one quarterly objective tied to a mission outcome is plenty. For example: "Families complete benefits applications without needing a second visit." Its key results should be outcome-flavored, not "ship feature X." Peer-reviewed critics warn that over-prescribed goals can narrow focus, increase risk-taking and unethical behavior, and crowd out learning [14]. Jeff Gothelf adds that routine, well-understood work often doesn't need OKRs at all [16].

7. **Present measures as learning, not judgment.** Muller's *The Tyranny of Metrics* (Princeton University Press) argues that metrics help most when they complement professional judgment rather than replace it [8]. When you share numbers with program staff, ask: "What does this tell us, and what are we missing?" Don't ask: "Why is your number down?" That keeps frontline staff, who hold the real context, on your side. It also matches the Performance Imperative's call for a culture that values learning [5].

- [ ] Add one row or arrow showing where your main product sits in that chain.
- [ ] List every metric you currently report and label each one as an output or an outcome.
- [ ] Run each candidate metric through CART and drop any that fail "Actionable" or "Responsible" [3].
- [ ] Choose one outcome measure plus one guardrail measure for the next quarter [5][13].
- [ ] Spend 20 minutes with your ED to check that the wording matches what funders already see.
- [ ] Ask one program staff member what the numbers would miss, and write down their answer.

## Where this advice breaks down

- **Logic models can oversimplify.** Evaluators have long warned that boxes and arrows suggest a neat, linear chain. Real programs are messier and loop back on themselves [17]. NPC's response is to treat a theory of change as a living document. Its advice is to build the model in stages and revise it as you learn [15]. Don't let the diagram become a reason to ignore surprises.
- **The goal-setting evidence is contested.** Locke and Latham, the leading goal-setting researchers, published a sharp rejoinder to the "Goals Gone Wild" critique. They argued that it relied on anecdotes and selective citations [18]. Goal setting is one of the most replicated findings in management research [14]. The fair reading is that specific goals do work, but they have real side effects when overused. Dose carefully.
- **OKR guidance comes mostly from large tech companies.** The evidence for OKRs is largely case-based and promotional [10][11]. Little rigorous research looks at OKRs in small nonprofits. Treat them as an optional format, not a requirement.
- **Funders may still want numbers you think are weak.** Ebrahim and Rangan's argument that small operators shouldn't chase systemic impact [19] is persuasive. Even so, a particular funder may require it. Negotiate scope politely, but don't fabricate attribution.
- **Some outcomes are slow.** Kellogg's guide expects longer-term outcomes over four to six years and impact over seven to ten [1]. A product sprint won't move them. Report leading indicators honestly as leading indicators.
