# Evidence as Influence: Continuous Discovery with Beneficiaries, Staff and Donors

> **In one paragraph:** As the only product manager in a small nonprofit, you rarely win arguments by title or budget. You win them by knowing, better than anyone, what is actually happening in the lives of the people your organization serves. Short, regular conversations that ask about real past experiences ("tell me about the last time you...") rather than opinions about the future give you that evidence. Mapping what you hear on an opportunity solution tree keeps it tied to a goal your Executive Director cares about. Bringing colleagues into the room means the insight belongs to everyone, not just you. And doing all of this with care for consent, power and people's time is not a nice extra. It is what makes the evidence trustworthy and the influence legitimate.

## Why this matters when you're the only PM

In a small nonprofit, decisions are often made by whoever speaks with the most confidence: a founder's memory of how things used to work, a board member's hunch, a funder's preferred program model. You can't out-rank any of those people. What you can do is bring something they don't have: fresh, specific stories from beneficiaries, frontline staff and donors, gathered steadily over time.

The sector has long recognized this gap. Writing in *Stanford Social Innovation Review*, Twersky, Buchanan and Threlfall noted that the people nonprofits serve rarely pay for services, so they have little leverage and their views are easy to overlook [14]. When you become the person who reliably brings their voice into planning meetings, you gain influence that is grounded in the mission rather than in office politics. That is the ethical kind of influence this repository is about.

## Practical advice

1. **Make discovery a small weekly habit, not a big annual project.** Teresa Torres, whose *Continuous Discovery Habits* has about 5,000 Goodreads ratings averaging 4.4, argues that good discovery teams engage with customers at least weekly, in small research activities tied to a clear outcome [1]. In a nonprofit, "customers" means beneficiaries, frontline staff (who are the main users of your internal tools) and donors. Thirty minutes a week is enough to start: one call with a program participant, one shadowing session at intake, or one chat with a recurring donor. Treat the weekly number as a practitioner rule of thumb, not a proven threshold (see "Where this advice breaks down").

2. **Ask about specific past events, never "would you...?"** Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (about 15,000 Goodreads ratings, 4.4 average) gives three widely cited rules: talk about their life instead of your idea, ask about specifics in the past instead of opinions about the future, and treat compliments as cheap [4]. Nielsen Norman Group's interviewing guidance agrees: ask about particular events rather than general processes, and avoid leading questions, which compromise data validity [5]. Torres adds that when asked about the future, people answer based on what they know they should do [3]. So instead of "Would you use a text reminder for your food pantry appointment?", ask "Tell me about the last time you missed or nearly missed an appointment. What happened?" Politeness bias is especially strong in nonprofits, where people may be grateful for your services and not want to seem critical.

3. **Map what you hear on an opportunity solution tree.** Torres's opportunity solution tree puts one desired outcome at the top (for example, "more families complete enrollment within two weeks"), the needs and pain points you heard in interviews beneath it, possible solutions beneath those, and small tests at the bottom [2]. She is firm that opportunities must come from interviews, not from guesses, and suggests updating the tree every three or four interviews [2]. This is your influence tool: when the board chair proposes a new app, you can show where that idea sits on the tree, which family stories it addresses and which it ignores, without saying "no" to anyone.

4. **Bring colleagues into the room so the insight is shared.** Insight you report secondhand is easy to dismiss. Insight a program manager heard with their own ears is hard to argue with. Torres recommends that the people building the product interview together [1]. IDEO.org's *Field Guide to Human-Centered Design* suggests up to three team members per interview with clear roles (interviewer, note-taker, observer) and interviewing people in their own setting [12]. Jared Spool reported that successful design teams had each member watch real users for at least two hours every six weeks [10], and the UK Government Digital Service adopted this "exposure hours" target across teams [11]. For you, that might mean inviting the Executive Director to sit in on one beneficiary call a month, or asking a development officer to join a donor interview.

5. **Talk to enough people, and to the right mix.** Jakob Nielsen's famous claim that five users find about 85% of usability problems was always about usability testing of comparable users, repeated in rounds [6]. For exploratory interviews, NN/g's Maria Rosala says five is often too few and recommends interviewing until new conversations stop changing your themes [8]. A peer-reviewed study of 60 interviews found that themes saturated within about twelve, with the main outlines visible after six [9]. Practically: start with five or six beneficiaries from one group, keep going until you stop hearing new things, and treat each distinct group (clients, volunteers, donors) as its own sample.

6. **Get real consent and reduce the power gap.** A client who depends on your food bank, shelter or legal clinic may feel they can't say no, or can't be honest. Fayard and Fathallah's *SSIR* critique warns that participation becomes extractive when communities share their stories, bear unpaid costs and never see change [17]. Twersky and colleagues stress assuring candor and confidentiality so respondents trust that honesty is safe [14]. In practice: say plainly that taking part (or saying no) will not affect their services; let them choose a familiar place or phone call; have someone other than their caseworker do the interview when possible; and store notes without names. Chicago Beyond's guidebook on research equity, aimed at funders, researchers and community groups, is a useful read on restoring communities as owners of what is learned [20].

7. **Pay people for their time.** In a widely cited *New England Journal of Medicine* framework, Gelinas and colleagues separated three reasons to pay participants: reimbursing costs, compensating time and burden, and offering an incentive; a 3ie summary notes they treat reimbursement and compensation as the fair default, and that cash is generally more respectful than in-kind gifts [19]. Fayard and Fathallah also recommend compensating participants [17]. A $25 gift card or prepaid card, plus bus fare or childcare, is a modest line item that funders increasingly understand. Check any rules your funders or local benefits programs have about cash payments.

8. **Close the loop, every time.** Listen4Good's research across hundreds of nonprofits found that organizations that told clients what they heard and what they would do saw feedback have more effect on culture and decision-making, and that closing the loop raises future response rates [15]. Fund for Shared Insight reported that nearly eight in ten of 112 early Listen for Good grantees used feedback to change services [16]. The "Design with people" principle in the Principles for Digital Development likewise calls for feedback channels that are regularly monitored and acted on [13]. A one-page "You said, we did" poster in the waiting room or a short donor email builds trust, and shows your ED and board that discovery leads to action.

9. **Turn stories into currency for leadership and funders.** Twersky and colleagues describe beneficiary perceptions as leading indicators that let leaders adjust before outcomes suffer [14]. Bring two or three anonymized quotes and one updated tree to each leadership meeting. When a funder asks how you know a program works, "Here is what 14 participants told us this quarter, and here is what we changed" is a stronger answer than an opinion.

## Try this in the next two weeks

- [ ] Pick one outcome your ED already cares about and write it at the top of a one-page opportunity solution tree.
- [ ] Draft a five-question interview guide where every question starts from a real past event ("Tell me about the last time...").
- [ ] Write a plain-language consent script that says participation will not affect services, explains what you'll do with notes, and offers a thank-you payment.
- [ ] Ask your ED for a small participant-payment budget (for example, 10 gift cards).
- [ ] Book three interviews: one beneficiary, one frontline staff member, one donor.
- [ ] Invite one colleague to each interview as note-taker.
- [ ] After the third interview, update the tree and share a half-page "what we heard" note with leadership.
- [ ] Tell participants what you heard and what happens next.

## Where this advice breaks down

- **The weekly cadence and "exposure hours" are practitioner claims.** Torres's weekly standard comes from her coaching experience [1], and Spool's six-week figure comes from his firm's unpublished research, which he admitted could be flawed [10]. They are useful targets, not proven thresholds. In a tiny organization, fortnightly may be what is sustainable.
- **"Five users" is often misapplied.** Faulkner's peer-reviewed study (cited about 380 times per Europe PMC) found that some random groups of five testers caught 99% of problems while others caught only 55%; ten users raised the worst case to 80% [7]. Small samples are fine for spotting problems in rounds, but not for claiming how common something is. Don't tell your board "most clients feel X" from six interviews.
- **Design thinking is not a cure for systemic problems.** Fayard and Fathallah argue that toolkits packaged as formulas ignore history, power and organizational culture, and that short grant cycles reward ideas over implementation [17]. Panthea Lee of Reboot argued that "user" is too narrow a frame in the social sector, where policymakers, frontline workers and institutions also shape outcomes, and recommended pairing user research with wider system and political analysis [18]. Interviews tell you about individual experience; they don't replace understanding funding rules, policy or root causes.
- **Feedback-to-outcome evidence is still thin.** The health and education studies cited in *SSIR* show correlations, not proof that listening causes better outcomes [14], and the Listen4Good figures are self-reported by participating organizations and published by the program's own leaders [15][16]. Present beneficiary feedback as one strong input, not as impact evaluation.
- **Some research needs more than good manners.** If you are working with children, people in crisis, survivors of violence or anyone whose legal status is at risk, informal interviewing may not be appropriate. Consider whether you need formal ethics review, trained staff or a partner organization.
- **Past behaviour doesn't cover everything.** For truly new services, people may have no past experience to describe. Use their workarounds and analogous experiences, then test small prototypes rather than asking hypothetical questions.

## AI study prompt

Paste the prompt below into an AI assistant to test and deepen what you've learned.

```text
I am the only product manager at a small nonprofit (few staff, limited budget, I report to an Executive Director, we have a board, we serve beneficiaries/clients and depend on donors and funders). I just read an article on "continuous discovery with beneficiaries, staff and donors, using evidence from real users as influence currency." Its key ideas were:

1. Make discovery a small, regular habit (roughly weekly, per Teresa Torres) rather than an annual project; the exact cadence is a practitioner rule of thumb.
2. Ask about specific past events ("Tell me about the last time...") rather than hypotheticals or opinions (The Mom Test; Nielsen Norman Group). Compliments and "I would" answers are weak evidence.
3. Map interview insights on an opportunity solution tree: one outcome at top, opportunities from real interviews, solutions, then small assumption tests. Update it every few interviews.
4. Involve colleagues directly (as note-takers or observers) so insight is shared; "exposure hours" of about two hours every six weeks is a practitioner benchmark.
5. "Five users" applies to iterative usability testing of similar users; exploratory interviews often need more (saturation near 12 in one peer-reviewed study), and small samples can't show prevalence.
6. With vulnerable people: make clear participation won't affect services, protect confidentiality, reduce power gaps (e.g., not their caseworker), and pay people for time and costs.
7. Close the loop: tell participants what you heard and what you will do; Listen4Good data (self-reported) links this to greater use of feedback.
8. Present beneficiary feedback as a leading indicator, not proof of impact.
9. Critiques: design thinking can ignore power, history and systems; pair interviews with wider system analysis and avoid extractive participation.

Please quiz me, ONE question at a time, and wait for my answer before continuing. Mix three types:
- Recall (e.g., "What are the three Mom Test rules?")
- Application to a scenario (e.g., rewrite a bad interview question)
- "What would you do?" judgment calls

After each answer: give me feedback, then the model answer with your reasoning. Adapt difficulty: harder if I'm doing well, simpler with hints if I struggle. Include at least one scenario involving all three of: an Executive Director, a board member, and a program staff member (for example, a board member pushing a new app idea, the ED wanting a quick answer for a funder, and a caseworker worried about clients feeling pressured to take part).

Ask about 8-10 questions. Then end with: a summary of my strengths and gaps, and 3 concrete follow-up actions I can take at my nonprofit in the next two weeks.

If you are unsure about a fact, a source, or a statistic, say so plainly rather than inventing one. Do not make up research findings, quotes or numbers.
```
